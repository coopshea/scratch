ok gas turbines. the thing I keep coming back to is that few machines anywhere are as optimized as a modern gas turbine and almost nobody outside the industry knows it. Bouchain in France hit 62.22% combined cycle efficiency in 2016, GE 9HA, it was a guinness record. the first power turbine at Neuchâtel in 1939 was around 17%. so where did the other 45 points come from?

my hunch is it's mostly one number: firing temperature. hotter gas in = more work out, basically Carnot. everything else is in service of letting the blades survive the heat. the gas going into the first stage is hotter than the melting point of the blades themselves. thats the crazy part, the metal is sitting in gas that would melt it.

how do they survive. single crystal blades, no grain boundaries so they don't creep. ceramic thermal barrier coatings. and the blades are hollow, compressor air pumped through them and out tiny holes so theres a film of cool air on the surface. each of these is worth like 100-200 degrees? need to check that

who gets credit?? Whittle and von Ohain get the jet engine, but the efficiency story is hundreds of metallurgists at Pratt and GE and NASA Lewis that nobody has heard of. VerSnyder at Pratt & Whitney did directional solidification in the 60s. maybe the honest answer is nobody, its an accumulation. which is sort of the point of the piece

objection to myself: maybe it's not temperature at all, maybe it's CFD and better compressor aerodynamics. pressure ratio matters too. i don't actually know the split.

how much further can it go? stoichiometric limit, eventually you run out of oxygen to burn. and NOx goes up with temperature, so emissions rules might cap it before physics does. could hydrogen change that
